---
title: Karaoke Sync — Lyrics in Time with the Audio
summary: A karaoke web app for live events. Audio and LRC lyrics are served from Cloudflare R2, lines follow the audio clock, and the microphone drives a waveform and a word-match score.
---

## Problem

A karaoke screen at a live event has three jobs. It must show the right line at the right moment. It must keep playing if my server has a bad minute mid-song. And it must not hand the audio files to anyone who has the address.

## Role

I built it alone, from the Next.js app to the Docker image.

## Architecture

The app runs on the Next.js App Router with TypeScript, and every page and API route sits behind sign-in. Audio and LRC lyric files live in Cloudflare R2. When a singer picks a track, one API route checks the session, applies a rate limit, validates the track against the catalogue and returns presigned URLs for the audio and the lyrics. That is the last backend call until the song ends.

In the browser, a small LRC parser turns each timestamp into milliseconds and sorts the lines. The stage reads the audio element's clock, picks the active line, and the `LyricsFlow` component scrolls to it. With the microphone on, a Web Audio analyser draws the waveform, and the browser's speech recognition matches sung words against the active line to produce a score.

## Decisions

- My own LRC parser instead of a library, because the format is one regular expression and I wanted to control the output, including two- and three-digit fractions.
- Presigned R2 URLs with a twelve-hour lifetime instead of proxying media through the app, because playback should not depend on my server and the link has to outlive the event.
- An in-memory rate limiter in the application layer instead of Redis, because the deployment is a single instance.
- An HTML5 audio element with my own files instead of the YouTube-based player of the first version, because third-party availability caused most of the early failures.

## Outcome

The app is live; the link opens on the sign-in page. Timestamps are parsed to the millisecond, and line changes follow the audio element's time updates, so real precision is that of the browser's clock events. What I learned: writing the rule "no backend calls during playback" first made the storage, auth and rate-limit choices follow.
